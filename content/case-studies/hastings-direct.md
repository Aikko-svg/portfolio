### 1. Bottom quartile, and nobody knew why

Industry benchmarking put Hastings Direct in the bottom quartile for start-to-quote conversion. 63% of users didn't finish the form, 38% of those who did still didn't get a quote, and the average feedback rating was 6.54. The assumption was that 42 questions was simply too many. A competitor asked 23.

::image full s1-before.jpg
The journey we inherited: a registration lookup that returns a wall of fields, a quote buried in plan tables, and the same action labelled differently from screen to screen.

The research said something more useful. I built the customer journey map with our UX researcher from eleven usability tests, customer interviews and a survey. Only 7 of the 11 testers reached a price, and it took them 17 minutes 53 seconds on average. But nobody complained about the number of questions. They complained that fields were grouped without logic, jargon wasn't explained, and the progress bar lied: "It says it has four steps, but it keeps adding and adding questions."

::image full s1-journey-map.jpg
A section of the journey map: the never-ending form, 23 questions against 42, and the drop when people fail to get a quote.

### 2. Underwriting said no

Our first proposal cut questions. Underwriting pushed back, and they were right: the questions that change a customer's risk profile are what make a quote cheaper. We won a handful of minor removals. Cutting the journey down properly was off the table.

So the question changed from "how many" to "how". I audited how direct and indirect competitors grouped the same questions, separated the groupings that made sense to customers from the ones that only existed because of a back-office system, and built alternative structures from them. We tested those with users to see which felt shortest for the same number of questions. The winner became three stages, Details, Cover and Quote, with a progress indicator that never adds a step. Jargon was rewritten with compliance in the room, and error messages started telling people how to fix things.

We brought Hastings' in-house UX team into decisions early too, so the people who'd own the journey after we left had shaped it.

::image full s2-structure.jpg
The new structure on desktop and mobile: Details, Cover, Quote. The indicator never adds a step, and the first thing the form does is return your car by name.

### 3. Six people looked in the same place

Partway through, a regulatory change meant customers had to be able to turn off auto-renewal. The client's instinct was to put it deep in a settings sub-menu. Renewals are revenue, so I understood why.

Rather than argue, I tested it. Six participants had to understand what auto-renewal was, then find it and turn it off on their own. Most couldn't. The convincing part was the pattern: asked where they expected it, almost all of them named the same place, and it wasn't where the client had put it.

I took the recordings and quotes to the director-level review. It wasn't my opinion against theirs; it was six customers looking in one spot. The client moved the control to where users had pointed.

### 4. Results

All of this ran inside six months, alongside My Account and live-site fixes. Keeping two rounds of usability testing in that schedule was deliberate, and the auto-renewal finding is why it paid off.

Six months after launch, the same customer survey showed reported ease of use up 33% and 14% more users saying they found what they needed. None of it came from cutting questions. It came from regrouping the same 42. Marketing also agreed to swap promotional messages at the quote reveal for reassurance, once they saw every tester say they'd drop off at the upsell page.

::image full s4-after.jpg
After: the same 42 questions as three clear steps. The car comes back by name, the indicator never adds a step, and the cheaper policy is pre-selected with the difference spelled out.
