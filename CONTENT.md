## Hastings Direct: narrative

**Hero image:** the "Found it!" vehicle confirmation screen, or the policy selection screen with the three-stage progress indicator.

**Section 1. The journey felt longer than it was.**

Hastings Direct was losing customers inside its quote journey. 63% of users were not completing the form and 38% never received a quote. The assumption going in was that 42 questions was simply too many.

Twelve usability tests and twelve customer interviews said otherwise. The number of questions was not the problem. Fields were grouped without logic, context was missing, and there was no sense of progress. One participant put it in a line that set the direction for the whole project: the journey felt even longer than it actually was.

*Image, full width:* customer journey map showing the full purchase flow with pain points and emotional curve. *Caption:* The map showed the problem was pacing and structure, not question count.

**Section 2. Compliance said no. That turned out to be the useful answer.**

Our first proposal cut questions. Compliance and underwriting pushed back: most of the questions were either legally required or needed to price the quote accurately, and could not move. There was a genuine back and forth, and we won a handful of removals, but they were marginal questions. The big ask, cutting the journey materially, was lost, and on the whole compliance were right.

That changed the design question from "how many" to "how". If the questions had to stay, the work was in how they were grouped, paced and explained. We introduced a visible three-stage indicator, Details, Cover, Quote, so users always knew where they were. Each section got a clear purpose. Error states were rewritten as recovery paths instead of dead ends.

We also brought the client's in-house UX team into decisions early, consulting them on how Hastings would approach each change before anything reached the directors. There was no friction to manage; the point was that the people who would own the journey after we left felt listened to and had shaped it.

*Image, two columns:* left, the "Found it!" registration screen; right, the progress indicator in position across three stages. *Caption:* Not a single mandatory question was cut. The journey got clearer, not shorter. The registration lookup is the detail I would point to: one field, and the car comes back named, so the first thing the form does is prove it already knows something about you. The progress indicator earned its place by being the one element that never moved, whatever else the screen was doing.

**Section 3. Six people looked in the same place. It was not there.**

Partway through, a regulatory change meant customers had to be able to switch off auto-renewal. The client's first instinct was to put the control deep inside a sub-menu in settings. Renewals are revenue, and the pull was understandable.

Rather than argue the point, I tested it. I built the task into the prototype: understand what auto-renewal is, then find it and turn it off, with no help from me. Most of the six participants could not find it. What made the finding persuasive was not the failure rate but the pattern: when asked where they expected it to be, almost everyone named the same place, and it was not where the client had put it.

I took the screen recordings, the frustration moments and the verbatim quotes back to the director-level review and made the case: this was not an opinion about hierarchy, it was six customers all looking in one spot. The client moved the control out of the buried sub-menu and into \[where users expected it, to confirm\]. On a regulated product, that is the difference between technically compliant and actually usable.

*Image, two columns:* left, a still from a test session or the task sheet; right, the before and after location of the auto-renew control. *Caption:* Evidence beats opinion in a room full of directors.

**Section 4. Pressure, parallel streams and the results.**

The engagement ran in stages under a six-month deadline, with the account manager and the client both holding us to dates. While the quote journey was in research, we were also fixing My Account and shipping corrections to the live site. Holding two rounds of usability testing inside that was a deliberate choice, and the auto-renewal finding is why it was worth it.

One argument we expected and did not get: marketing agreed quickly to replace promotional messaging at the quote reveal with reassurance and social proof, once they saw the anxiety data from the interviews.

Six months after launch, Hastings Direct reported its all-time NPS high for digital sales. Reported ease of use rose 33% and task success 14%. None of it came from removing questions. It came from restructuring how the same questions were presented, and from one room full of directors watching six customers fail the same task.

*Image, full width:* the policy comparison screen with progressive disclosure, desktop and mobile. *Caption:* The same 42 questions, restructured around how people actually decide.

## Utility Warehouse: narrative

**Hero image:** the structured ticket submission view with guided fault entry and inline validation, or the split-panel ticket status screen.

**Section 1. A ticket that came back the next day.**

A first-line adviser at Utility Warehouse took a call about a fault, filled in the legacy BILL form, and raised it to second line. The form was built on pre-selected templates with outdated questions and no validation, so nothing stopped an incomplete ticket going through. A day later, sometimes sooner, it came back marked incorrectly raised.

The adviser then had to ring the customer again, explain that an engineer was not coming yet and that more checks were needed, and ask questions the customer believed they had already answered. The customer wanted it fixed yesterday. The adviser took the anger for a system that had given them no way to get it right first time.

At the business level, the head of second line, who managed external engineer dispatch and reported on fines, put the previous year's cost of misdispatched engineers at £1 million. Over 40% of fault submissions needed manual correction. Six separate escalation pathways were applied inconsistently. It looked like a training problem. It was an architecture problem.

*Image, full width:* the legacy BILL fault form, dense, template-driven, manual fault codes. *Caption:* No validation, no guidance, no way to know whether a ticket was complete before it left your screen.

**Section 2. The fight over like-for-like.**

Utility Warehouse was migrating from BILL to William, a new cloud platform. The programme lead and a director-level sponsor wanted the existing fault workflows replicated in William on the faster timeline a straight copy allowed. The migration deadline was real and the argument was reasonable.

As a design team we pushed back: copying the workflow would copy the £1 million. The migration was the one moment the fault architecture could be rebuilt from first principles, and that window would not come again. We did not win it clean. \[What was conceded to get agreement: scope, timeline, a phased rollout? To confirm.\] But the principle held: William would get a redesigned fault and triage flow, not a lift and shift.

*Image, two columns:* left, the end-to-end operational journey map; right, the six legacy escalation pathways side by side. *Caption:* Six routes to second line, each with its own owner and its own logic.

**Section 3. We got the process wrong, then fixed it.**

Our first approach was to consult each department separately: first line, then second line, then the next. It did not work. Feedback from one group contradicted the last, and every round trip meant going back to a department that had already signed off and reopening the decision.

So we changed how we worked. We ran a single workshop with representatives from every part of the business the fault flow touched: first-line advisers, second-line engineers, the head of second line, and Learning and Development. Decisions were made in the room with the people who would live with them, and alignment that had taken weeks took an afternoon.

Bringing L&D in was the decision that mattered most and the one we nearly missed. New advisers spent around a week in a classroom before taking live calls, and L&D owned that programme. Designing the new fault flow with them meant the training was rebuilt alongside the product. Advisers learned one system in the classroom and met the same system on the floor.

We then ran smaller workshops with the advisers themselves, not to train them but to show what a validated, guided flow meant for their day: raise it right, answer every question, and the ticket does not come back. For a group used to taking the call-back, that landed.

*Image, two columns:* left, a workshop artefact or affinity wall; right, the validation rules mapped against the second-line checklist. *Caption:* One room, every department, decisions made once.

**Section 4. Structure in, errors out.**

The fault-raising and triage template was mine. I took the contradictory requirements from first line, second line and L&D and restructured them into one guided fault entry: progressive questions driven by the fault type, inline validation on every required field, and a single structured escalation path replacing the previous six. I tested each iteration with first-line advisers before it went anywhere near the platform engineers, then worked with them through implementation. A split-panel ticket status screen gave advisers visibility of where a ticket was and whether it was complete, which the legacy system had never offered.

Within three months, engineer misdispatch costs had fallen by £184K, reported by the same head of second line who had tracked the fines. Ticket quality errors reaching second line dropped 56%. Average handling time on fault calls fell 17%, because advisers were no longer re-raising the same fault twice.

The interface changed. What actually fixed it was the architecture underneath, and the decision to design the training with the product rather than after it.

*Image, full width:* the William dashboard end state. *Caption:* Fewer errors, no call-backs, one escalation path. The detail worth looking at is the validation: each question is checked against what second line actually needs to dispatch, so the ticket cannot be raised until it is complete, and the adviser sees why a field matters at the moment they are filling it in, not in a rejection the next day.

## PMI: narrative

**Hero image:** the platform in two markets, same components, different hierarchy.

**Section 1. Five markets, one platform, and Germany did not need it.**

PMI's B2B retailer platform had grown differently in every European market. Head office wanted one system: a fixed set of components that each market could reuse and reorder for its own catalogue, since products, popularity and even what was sold varied by country.

Most markets were in need and open to it. Germany was not. It was the most successful market in Europe, already had its own way of doing things, and had little reason to want a platform built for everyone else. Its site was not exceptional, but it worked, and the stance was reasonable: if the new one is not better, it should not replace ours.

*Image, full width:* the before state across three or four markets. *Caption:* The same products, a different platform in every country.

**Section 2. Sign-off was easy until it was not.**

The first phase moved fast. Components went through review on both sides with little resistance. When I asked the German team for input on what they needed, the interest was not there. Then, with the component set largely designed, Germany came back hard: this is not good enough, we need more.

The frustration was real. But the actual problem was not Germany. It was that head office had never said what the limits were. They would tell us something was out of scope, then, when a market pushed, concede it after all. We were stuck between the client paying for the work and the market that was often right, with no fixed line to design against.

So I called a hardball session with head office with one question: what are the non-negotiables? Not preferences, the actual no-gos. The answer was smaller than expected. The constraint was build complexity and timeline, not brand or governance. Once that was written down, Germany's requests could be assessed against something real, and most of them fit.

Germany became the proxy for every market. Its requirements were the heaviest and the other markets were content to let it lead. The variants it won went into the shared system for everyone. The first release ended up more established than head office had scoped, because the room to push was there all along. Nobody had named it. The cost was that smaller markets inherited variants they did not need and a heavier system than they would have chosen; letting the biggest market set the ceiling was the right call for the programme, not for every market in it.

*Image, two columns:* left, the component library; right, the variants originally scoped for phase two, now in release one. *Caption:* The limit was build effort, not brand. Once named, there was more room than anyone assumed.

**Section 3. IQOS wanted the top menu. Compliance said some products could not sit together.**

The information architecture had its own politics. The IQOS brand team wanted their products in primary navigation, not behind a sub-category, and had their own commercial reasons. Compliance ruled that certain product groupings could not appear together regardless of what any market or brand wanted. Each market had its own view of the hierarchy that suited its catalogue.

The approach was the same as with components. Get everyone on one call, walk through the high-level IA together, and use the research to argue the groupings. We had done a lot of it and it let us push back on a few requests with evidence rather than opinion. Compliance set the hard boundaries; research decided the rest; the markets kept the freedom to reorder within that.

*Image, two columns:* left, the final IA with compliance constraints marked; right, two markets' hierarchies built from it. *Caption:* Compliance drew the boundaries. Research settled the arguments inside them. The detail: the same homepage dashboard component carries a different task hierarchy in every market, because the order of the cards is a market-level variable and the card itself is not. That is what let fourteen markets ship from one system without fourteen designs.

**Section 4. Results.**

Time on platform rose 32% and task submission 12.6% across the unified platform. The five launch markets went live on one component system, each with its own hierarchy, and it has since been rolled out to fourteen. The design decisions mattered, but the one that changed the project was forcing the client to write down its constraints. Everything got easier after that.

*Image, full width:* the live platform in two markets side by side. *Caption:* One system, fourteen hierarchies.

## Lucky Riches: narrative

**Hero image:** the Cashier deposit screen on mobile with the behaviour-spec column beside it, or the Missions hub across its four tier colours.

**Section 1. Not a reskin.**

Most white-label casinos are a logo and a colour swap on the provider's platform. Lucky Riches was different because the investors owned the provider, Terragon, and wanted a product that was theirs. That changed the brief from "skin this" to "design this", and it changed what the developers were prepared to do: where the platform's components did not fit the design, Terragon rebuilt them.

I was brought in on a fixed fee to lead design end to end, from discovery through to QA sign-off. The working group was a product manager, a data analyst, the marketing team, Terragon's developers and, for anything that touched money, an off-site payment provider and compliance. Eleven product flows were in scope: sign-in and password recovery, registration, home, search, bonuses, cashier, in-game, error states, missions, my account and an affiliate landing page. \[Confirm timeline and the Ontario/AGCO regulatory context if that is the licensed market.\]

*Image, full width:* the handoff file's page list. *Caption:* Eleven flows, 250 screens, 21 behaviour specs. The numbers are the deliverable, not the story.

**Section 2. One deposit flow, not two.**

The deposit was the hardest piece because it did not belong to any one party. The screens were ours, the shell was Terragon's, and the moment a player confirmed an amount they went off-site to the payment provider. That provider had to be brought up to date on what we were building, shown the flow, and persuaded it could support what we needed inside it. A lot of the design work on Cashier was negotiation.

Into that, the product manager proposed a quick-deposit pop-up for returning players: a different modal with their previous card already selected. On paper it was a conversion win. In practice it meant two parallel deposit journeys to design, build and keep in sync, and a third branch the moment a returning player wanted to add a card we had not saved. I argued for one flow instead, with whatever method the player used last pre-selected on entry. New players and returning players would walk the same path and the pre-selection would do the quick-deposit job without a second build. That is what shipped, with the withdrawal reversal surfaced inside the same flow so a player with a pending withdrawal could cancel it and play without leaving the cashier.

*Image, two across:* left, the PM's two-journey sketch; right, the single flow as shipped with the saved method pre-selected. *Caption:* One journey, one set of transactional states, one thing for Terragon and the payment provider to test.

**Section 3. The spec is the design.**

Because the developers were rebuilding components rather than reusing them, screens alone would not have been enough. Every flow shipped with a behaviour-spec document of numbered rules: 21 documents and roughly 480 rules across the file, 86 of them for Cashier alone, covering transactional states from "connecting" through "card expired" to "pending review". Sign-in uses a generic authentication error so the form cannot be used to enumerate accounts. Password reset is rate-limited invisibly. In-game, the rule is that the game never stops: search and deposit open as overlays with play continuing behind them.

The design system underneath is tokenised and aligned to shadcn and Tailwind so that a variant in Figma maps to a variant in code. Missions is the clearest example of why that mattered: one tier colour token drives every component in the hub across four tiers, the countdown switches to an hh:mm:ss urgent state under 24 hours and fires the failed modal itself at zero, and the six-page mobile explainer condenses to three on desktop. All of that is a handful of rules, not a hundred extra screens.

*Image, two across:* left, the Cashier spec document; right, the Missions hub in two tier colours. *Caption:* 86 rules for the money, one colour token for the game.

**Section 4. Compliance you can see.**

Regulated gambling puts responsible-play and verification requirements on the product that most apps never face, and I treated them as design problems rather than legal copy. Verification is tiered and risk-triggered: four levels, each with a Verified, Required, Pending, Optional or Not-required-yet status, with identity, proof of payment and source-of-wealth checks switched on by operator-configurable risk rules. Players can register and deposit before full verification but are gated from play and withdrawal until their profile is complete, and when verification is done the success screen sends them back to wherever they came from, game, withdrawal or hub. Take a Break confirms, logs the player out and suspends the account with start and end times computed in real time. Restricted territories never appear in the country index at registration.

The registration-to-cashier redirect is the one I lost. I pushed back on dropping a new player straight into the cashier before they had seen a game. The product manager had conversion data from another platform in the group showing it was the best-performing flow, and the evidence was better than my instinct. What I got in exchange was the shape of verification: registration asks the minimum, the deposit comes first, and the full profile and documents are only demanded at withdrawal, when the player is already invested.
